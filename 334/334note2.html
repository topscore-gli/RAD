Radiography Viva Master Guide • Positioning • Technique • Image Criteria

📖 How to Use This Guide

Learn the exact figures stated in your course material first. Then study the clearly labelled supplementary reference notes, which help explain routine practice and viva reasoning. Where departments differ, state that you would follow the local protocol and exposure chart.

This is an educational viva aid, not a substitute for your department’s protocol, a supervising radiographer, or a radiologist’s request. Patient condition, equipment, detector, grid, body habitus and local practice affect technique selection.

2️⃣ Routine PA Chest: Positioning and Technique

Purpose

  • To assess the lungs, pleura, heart size and contour, mediastinum, hila, diaphragm, visible ribs and thoracic bony structures.
  • PA erect is preferred for routine chest imaging when the patient can safely stand or sit upright because it reduces magnification of the heart compared with an AP projection.
  • Common indications include cough, fever, dyspnoea, suspected infection/consolidation, pleural effusion, pneumothorax and general chest assessment when clinically requested.

Patient and Equipment Positioning

  • Verify the request and patient identity; explain the examination and check relevant pregnancy status according to local policy.
  • Remove necklaces, clothing fasteners and other radiopaque objects from the chest region where feasible; provide a gown and preserve dignity.
  • Position the patient erect facing the image receptor, chest against it, with the median sagittal plane centred and not rotated.
  • Place hands on hips with palms out or wrap arms around the receptor; roll shoulders forward to move the scapulae away from the lung fields.
  • Raise the chin clear of the lung apices. Keep the shoulders level and ensure the chest is close to the receptor.

Centring and Beam

  • Course figure: centre at the level of T8 / spinous process of T7, coincident with the lung midpoint; inferior angle of scapula is the stated surface landmark.
  • Supplementary routine reference: many departments centre the CR perpendicular to the midline at approximately T7. If your lecturer expects the course wording, give that wording first.
  • CR is usually perpendicular to the detector for the routine PA view; do not angle it unless a specific protocol requires it.
  • FFD/SID from the notes: 150–180 cm. Use the department’s specified distance.

Breathing Instruction

  • Ask the patient to take a deep breath in, then hold it. Make the exposure at full inspiration.
  • A good inspiratory image generally shows about 10 posterior ribs above the diaphragm, although patient condition and anatomy affect this sign.
  • Clear instruction and rehearsal help reduce motion and improve lung expansion.

Exposure Factors

Course figures: 70–80 kVp, 8–12 mAs. Do not silently replace these when answering a question about what the notes say.
Supplementary practice note: many modern digital chest protocols use higher kVp and lower mAs, often around 110–125 kVp, but exact values vary. In clinical practice, use the approved departmental chart and exposure index guidance.

Image Evaluation: What a Good PA Chest Should Show

  • Both lungs from apices to costophrenic angles are included; the lateral chest walls are not cut off.
  • The patient is not rotated: medial ends of the clavicles are approximately equidistant from the spinous processes.
  • Scapulae are largely outside the lung fields; clavicles are not excessively high or low.
  • Adequate inspiration and no significant respiratory motion.
  • Exposure permits assessment of lung markings and mediastinal structures without excessive noise or overexposure.
  • The heart and mediastinum are not disproportionately magnified; no anatomy is clipped and the side marker is visible without obscuring anatomy.

3️⃣ Lateral Chest

Purpose

  • Provides a second projection to localise abnormalities and assess areas hidden by the heart, diaphragm or overlapping structures.
  • Used with PA chest when clinically indicated; the left lateral is commonly performed to keep the heart closer to the detector and reduce magnification.

Positioning and Centring (Supplementary Reference)

  • Position the patient in a true lateral stance with the side of the chest against the receptor, arms raised above the head and chin clear of the lung fields.
  • Avoid rotation: shoulders and hips should be stacked, and the posterior ribs should be nearly superimposed.
  • A common reference centring level is around T7 at the midcoronal plane, with the CR perpendicular to the receptor.
  • A long SID, commonly around 180 cm, is often used. The course notes do not state lateral-view exposure factors.
  • Expose on full inspiration and include both apices and both costophrenic angles.

Image Evaluation

  • Both lungs and the entire thorax are included from apices to costophrenic angles.
  • Posterior ribs are close to superimposed; excessive separation suggests rotation.
  • Arms do not obscure the upper lungs; the diaphragm and retrocardiac region are assessable.
  • There is no significant motion, clipping or avoidable artefact.

AP chest is not interchangeable with PA chest. Always state the patient’s position and projection, and explain why a portable/AP view may be necessary.

6️⃣ Collimation, Markers and Image Quality

Collimation

  • Collimate to include the entire required chest anatomy: apices superiorly, costophrenic angles inferiorly and the lateral chest walls.
  • Set the field before exposure and verify that no required anatomy will be cut off. Adjust to the patient’s size and projection.
  • Tight collimation reduces unnecessary irradiation and scatter, but over-collimation can exclude important anatomy.

Side Markers and Patient Identification

  • Place the correct anatomical side marker before exposure, ideally within the collimated field but not obscuring anatomy.
  • Check patient identity, requested projection and any relevant previous images according to departmental workflow.
  • Do not rely on post-processing to correct a missing or incorrect marker.

Grid, Geometry and Scatter

  • A grid may be used for routine adult chest imaging according to local protocol to reduce scatter; small children and selected patients may not require the same grid approach.
  • A longer SID reduces magnification and helps standardise chest geometry.
  • High-kVp technique provides useful penetration through the mediastinum while retaining lung detail; mAs must be sufficient to avoid excessive quantum noise but should be optimised to the protocol.
  • Avoid repeat exposures by checking positioning, field coverage, marker placement and breathing instructions before exposure.

7️⃣ Patient Preparation, Safety and Breathing

  • Confirm: patient identity, request, clinical indication and correct projection; explain the procedure in simple language.
  • Pregnancy: follow local pregnancy-screening and justification policy; do not make assumptions based on appearance.
  • Metal and clothing: remove external objects from the chest field when possible and provide a gown.
  • Infection control: hand hygiene and appropriate cleaning/PPE for the setting, especially for portable examinations.
  • Mobility and trauma: do not force a patient to stand, raise arms or rotate if unsafe. Follow trauma/portable protocols and obtain help when needed.
  • Lines and devices: do not disconnect oxygen, drains, IV lines or monitoring devices without authorisation; coordinate movement with clinical staff.
  • Breathing: give one clear instruction, rehearse if needed, and expose on the requested phase. If the patient cannot cooperate, adapt to the clinical situation and document/follow protocol.

Important distinction

A fall from height with suspected thoracic vertebral injury is a thoracic-spine examination, not automatically a chest X-ray. A suspected sternum injury may require a sternum-focused study. Do not confuse these with routine chest projections.

🔟 Rapid Viva Answers

Q. What is the routine chest projection?
A. PA chest, when the patient can safely be positioned erect.

Q. What is the FFD in the course notes?
A. 150–180 cm.

Q. What is the course centring point?
A. Level of T8 / spinous process of T7, coincident with the lung midpoint; inferior angle of scapula is the stated landmark.

Q. What exposure factors are written in the course notes?
A. 70–80 kVp and 8–12 mAs.

Q. What cassette size is listed?
A. 17 × 14 inches.

Q. Why is PA preferred to AP for routine chest imaging?
A. The heart is closer to the receptor, reducing magnification; scapulae can also be moved out of the lung fields.

Q. Why ask the patient to take a deep breath?
A. To expand the lungs and improve visualisation of the lung fields.

Q. Why raise the chin?
A. To prevent the chin from obscuring the lung apices.

Q. Why roll the shoulders forward?
A. To move the scapulae laterally away from the lung fields.

Q. Why use a long SID?
A. To reduce magnification and improve standardisation of chest geometry.

Q. What shows rotation on a PA chest?
A. The medial ends of the clavicles are not equidistant from the spinous processes.

Q. What anatomy must be included?
A. Both apices, both lungs, lateral chest walls and both costophrenic angles.

Q. What is the purpose of a lateral chest?
A. It provides a second projection and helps localise findings obscured on the frontal image.

Q. What if the patient cannot stand?
A. Use the appropriate AP portable/erect/semi-erect method as clinically safe and according to local protocol.

Q. What should you do if the patient is unstable or has drains/lines?
A. Do not force movement or disconnect devices; follow the clinical team’s safety instructions and portable/trauma protocol.

1️⃣1️⃣ Practice Questions and Model Answers

  1. Describe the PA chest position from preparation to exposure.
    Model answer: Confirm identity/request, explain, prepare the chest, position erect facing the receptor, midline centred and not rotated, chin raised, shoulders rolled forward, centre at the course-specified level, collimate to include apices through costophrenic angles, place marker, ask for full inspiration and breath-hold, expose using the approved chart.
  2. State the course technique factors.
    Model answer: FFD 150–180 cm; centring at T8 / spinous process of T7 with inferior angle of scapula as landmark; 70–80 kVp at 8–12 mAs; cassette 17 × 14 inches.
  3. Why can an AP chest make the heart look larger?
    Model answer: The heart is farther from the detector in AP geometry, so magnification is greater than in a PA projection.
  4. Give five image-quality criteria for a PA chest.
    Model answer: Full anatomy included, no rotation, scapulae mostly outside lungs, adequate inspiration, no motion, adequate penetration/exposure and correct marker.
  5. What would you do if a patient cannot hold their breath?
    Model answer: Explain and coach if possible; adapt to the patient’s condition and use the approved protocol. Do not compromise safety to obtain an ideal breath-hold.
  6. How does a lateral view complement PA?
    Model answer: It helps localise lesions and assesses retrocardiac, retrosternal and posterior regions that may be obscured on the frontal image.
  7. What is the difference between a chest and thoracic-spine examination?
    Model answer: Chest imaging evaluates thoracic organs and chest structures; thoracic-spine views specifically assess the vertebral column and require a separate request/protocol.
  8. Why is collimation important?
    Model answer: It limits the irradiated area, reduces scatter and unnecessary exposure, and improves image quality while retaining all required anatomy.
  9. What factors influence exposure selection?
    Model answer: Patient size, projection, detector, grid, equipment, clinical condition and departmental exposure chart.
  10. What should you say if asked for lateral exposure factors not present in your notes?
    Model answer: The supplied notes do not state them. I would use the department’s approved technique chart rather than invent a figure.

1️⃣2️⃣ Last-Minute Revision Checklist

  • Memorise the course values exactly: PA chest; FFD 150–180 cm; centring T8 / spinous process T7 with inferior angle of scapula landmark; 70–80 kVp; 8–12 mAs; 17 × 14 inches.
  • Know the routine PA position and why the chin, shoulders, midline and inspiration matter.
  • Be able to evaluate rotation, inspiration, scapular overlap, collimation, marker placement, motion and exposure.
  • Know the purpose and limitations of lateral, AP erect, AP semi-erect, AP supine, apical/lordotic and expiration views.
  • Practise describing patient preparation, safety, and what to do when the patient cannot stand or cooperate.
  • For values missing from the course notes, say so and refer to the department’s protocol. Do not invent exact factors.
  • Answer in a consistent sequence: indication → patient preparation → position → centring/CR → collimation → exposure/breathing → image criteria → safety.

Suggested answer structure for any projection:
1. Name the view and indication.
2. Prepare and position the patient.
3. State the centring point and CR direction.
4. State collimation and SID/FFD.
5. Give the exposure factors exactly as taught or refer to the approved chart if absent.
6. Explain breathing instructions.
7. State image-quality criteria and radiation-safety precautions.
