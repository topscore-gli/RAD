RAD 312

⚡ Exposure Factor Selection

kV Selection • Autotransformer • Variac • Voltage Drop & Compensation

📋 One-Sentence Summary

Exposure-factor selection involves choosing kV, mA and time. Kilovoltage is controlled through the autotransformer (and sometimes a Variac), then stepped up, while anticipated transformer, rectification and tube-current voltage losses are compensated for so that the desired voltage is ultimately available across the X-ray tube.

🧠 Big Picture Sequence

kV → AUTOTRANSFORMER → STEP-UP → LOSSES → COMPENSATION → kV INDICATION

1️⃣ What are Exposure Factors?

When an X-ray exposure is made, the operator selects certain factors to control the production of X-rays. The three main exposure factors are:

  • Kilovoltage (kV) – controls the potential difference across the X-ray tube and strongly affects X-ray photon energy and penetrating power
  • Milliamperage (mA) – controls the tube current and therefore the quantity of electrons flowing through the tube
  • Exposure time – determines how long the tube current flows

This material concentrates mainly on kV selection. (mA selection was dealt with separately in the filament-circuit material.)

🧠 Analogy

Think of an X-ray tube like a system in which electrons are being pushed from one side to another.
kV = how strongly the electrons are pushed
mA = how many electrons are flowing
Time = how long the flow continues
So kV controls the energy/penetrating ability, while mA and time are associated with the amount of radiation produced.

2️⃣ X-rays in the Electromagnetic Spectrum

X-rays occupy a region of the electromagnetic spectrum with wavelengths of approximately 0.01–10 μm and photon energies of approximately 100 eV–100 keV.

The material divides X-rays broadly into:

  • Soft X-rays – lower-energy end. Used in X-ray crystallography and mammography
  • Hard X-rays – higher-energy end. Used in routine radiography, radiotherapy and airport baggage inspection

3️⃣ What Does Kilovoltage Mean?

When we select a particular kV we are determining the potential difference between the cathode and anode of the X-ray tube. This potential difference accelerates electrons from the cathode toward the anode.

Important relationship:
Higher kV → greater electron acceleration → greater electron kinetic energy → potentially higher-energy X-ray photons.
The maximum energy of an X-ray photon is limited by the kinetic energy of the electron striking the anode.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine throwing a ball at a wall.
If you throw it gently, it reaches the wall with relatively little energy.
If you throw it very fast, it hits the wall with much more energy.
Similarly: Low kV → electrons have less energy; High kV → electrons have more energy.

Effect of kV on radiographic penetration

  • Low kV – X-rays have relatively lower energy and penetration. If too low the radiograph may be underpenetrated.
  • High kV – X-rays have greater penetrating ability. If excessively high the radiograph may be overpenetrated.
🧠 Easy memory aid

kV = penetrating power
Higher kV → stronger X-ray beam → greater penetration.

4️⃣ kV Selector and Autotransformer

The kV selector is located at the autotransformer. The selector consists of different tapings from the transformer winding.

Basic arrangement:
AC supply → Autotransformer (Major + Minor kV selectors) → Step-up transformer → High voltage → X-ray tube

What is an autotransformer?

An autotransformer is a transformer with one continuous winding that serves as both the primary and secondary winding. This differs from an ordinary two-winding transformer.

Because X-ray equipment works with very high voltages, it is safer to control the voltage before it is stepped up to the very high voltage required by the X-ray tube.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine carrying a large amount of water. It is much easier and safer to control the flow while the water is still at low pressure than after it has been converted to extremely high pressure. Similarly, voltage is adjusted before it is stepped up to tens of kilovolts.

Transformer turns ratio

The relationship between voltage and number of turns is:

Wp / Ws = Vp / Vs

Where Wp = number of primary turns, Ws = number of secondary turns, Vp = primary voltage, Vs = secondary voltage.

Worked example

Input voltage = 415 V
Total winding = 1000 turns
Desired selected voltage = 200 V

Ws = (Wp × Vs) / Vp = (1000 × 200) / 415 ≈ 482 turns

Therefore approximately the 482nd turn is selected to obtain 200 V.

Stepping up the voltage

The selected 200 V is then supplied to a step-up transformer to produce 40 kV.

Ratio = 200 : 40 000 = 1 : 200

Therefore for every 1 turn on the primary there are 200 turns on the secondary.

415 V mains → Autotransformer (1000 turns) → select 482nd turn → 200 V → Step-up transformer (1 : 200) → 40 kV

Major and minor kV selectors

  • Major kV selector – typically changes the kV in larger steps (example given: 10 kV)
  • Minor kV selector – typically changes the kV in smaller steps (example given: 1 kV)

The difference is essentially the spacing between the transformer tap points.

Example of simplified operation:

  • Major: 40 → 50 → 60 → 70 kV
  • Minor: 40 → 41 → 42 → 43 kV

5️⃣ Variac Transformer

Ordinary tapped autotransformers select voltage at fixed points. Some procedures (particularly fluoroscopy) require the voltage to be changed smoothly during an exposure. A fixed-tap system cannot provide continuous voltage adjustment. Therefore a special type of autotransformer called a Variac transformer (variable voltage autotransformer) is used.

How does a Variac work?

Instead of having fixed taps, the Variac uses a carbon brush slider that moves across the transformer winding. As the slider moves, the effective number of turns changes and the output voltage changes continuously.

🧠 Analogy – Staircase vs Ramp

Ordinary tapped autotransformer is like a staircase: you can stand only on specific steps (40, 50, 60, 70 kV).
Variac is like a ramp: the slider can move continuously so you can adjust the voltage smoothly between values.

Construction of the Variac

  • A round magnetic core
  • Windings around the core
  • A carbon brush slider

The primary connection is made from both ends of the winding. The secondary connection uses one end of the winding and the carbon brush position. Moving the brush changes the effective turns ratio.

6️⃣ Does Selected kV Remain Constant?

No. Suppose we select 40 kV. We might expect exactly 40 kV to appear across the X-ray tube throughout the exposure. However, the actual voltage can fall for three reasons:

1. Transformer losses

An ideal transformer assumes no energy losses. Real transformers are not ideal. Energy can be lost through eddy currents, hysteresis, etc. Some electrical energy is converted into heat. Therefore the actual voltage obtained will not necessarily equal the theoretical voltage calculated from the transformer equation. The actual result depends partly on the transformer’s rating.

🧠 Analogy

Suppose you put ₦100 into a process but only ₦95 comes out because ₦5 is lost through friction. Similarly: Input electrical energy → some losses → slightly lower useful output.

2. Effect of tube current

From the power relationship P = I × V, therefore V = P / I. If power is considered constant, an increase in current is associated with a reduction in voltage.

In an X-ray tube the selected mA determines the tube current. Increasing mA means more electrons are released by the filament, more electrons travel from cathode to anode, and greater tube current flows. A voltage drop occurs. The material gives an example where a selected 40 kV could fall to approximately 36 kV once the tube is energized.

🧠 Analogy

Think of a road carrying vehicles. Voltage is like the driving force. Current is like the number of vehicles moving. Increasing the number of vehicles creates more demand on the system and can result in a greater voltage drop.

3. Rectification loss

The X-ray generator contains rectifiers. The material treats the rectification process as introducing additional resistance. When current passes through this part of the circuit a further voltage reduction occurs. Therefore rectification contributes to the total voltage loss.

7️⃣ Total Voltage Drop and Compensation

The material summarizes the total voltage drop as:

Voltage drop = Transformer loss + Rectification loss + Tube-current loss

🧠 Memory aid

Think: T + R + T
T = Transformer
R = Rectification
T = Tube current
Together they cause the voltage drop.

How do we compensate for voltage drop?

The solution is to initially select a voltage higher than the desired final voltage.

Example:

  • Desired tube voltage = 40 kV
  • Expected voltage loss = 10 kV
  • Therefore the system should initially provide 40 + 10 = 50 kV
  • After the expected losses: 50 – 10 = 40 kV

So the tube receives approximately the required 40 kV. This is achieved by selecting more turns on the autotransformer to compensate for expected losses.

🧠 Analogy

Suppose you need to receive ₦40,000 but know that ₦10,000 will be lost during a process. You would have to start with ₦50,000 so that after losing ₦10,000, ₦40,000 remains. The same principle is applied to voltage compensation.

Why must mA be considered?

Transformer losses and rectification losses are usually relatively constant for a given system. However, tube-current loss changes with the selected mA. Different examinations require different mA values. Therefore the compensation system needs to account for the mA selected.

kV compensator ganged to the mA selector

One solution is to connect another kV selector mechanically or electrically with the mA selector (described as being “ganged” to the mA selector).

  • When mA is increased → tube current increases → expected voltage drop increases → compensating kV selector increases the number of autotransformer turns → more voltage is supplied initially → expected loss is compensated
  • Lower mA → lower expected voltage drop → less compensation required

Key idea: As mA increases, compensation increases.

8️⃣ Kilovoltage Indicator

The radiographer needs to know the kV being selected. However, at the time of selection the selected voltage has not yet been applied across the X-ray tube. The system therefore takes expected voltage losses into account (tube current, rectification, and energy losses in the autotransformer and high-tension transformer).

Two methods of displaying kV are described:

1. Calibrated autotransformer

The kV selector knob is marked with kV values. The number indicated on the knob does not simply represent the raw voltage selected from the transformer. Instead it is calibrated to account for expected voltage drops.

Example: Required tube voltage = 40 kV. The system may need an additional primary voltage of 40 V. Therefore instead of selecting exactly 200 V the system selects 200 + 40 = 240 V. The point corresponding to 240 V on the autotransformer is then labelled as 40 kV. After the expected losses occur, this corresponds to the required tube voltage.

2. Pre-reading kilovolt meter

A voltmeter is connected to the kV selector but is specially calibrated. Its reading represents the expected actual kV across the X-ray tube after taking the expected voltage drop into account. The operator does not have to calculate the losses manually each time.

⭐ Key Concepts to Remember

  • kV controls the penetrating power / energy of the X-ray beam
  • Higher kV → higher electron energy → higher-energy X-rays → greater penetration
  • Autotransformer allows voltage to be controlled before it is stepped up
  • Major selector = larger increments; Minor selector = smaller increments
  • Variac = variable voltage autotransformer with carbon brush slider for continuous adjustment
  • Actual tube kV falls because of: Transformer losses + Rectification losses + Tube-current losses
  • Total voltage drop equation: VD = TL + RL + TCL
  • Compensation = initially select a higher voltage than the desired final tube voltage
  • mA affects tube-current loss, so compensation can be ganged to the mA selector
  • Two methods of kV indication: Calibrated autotransformer and Pre-reading kilovolt meter
