RAD 312

⚡ Exposure Switches & Switching Devices

Timers • Mechanical Contactors • Electronic Switches • Thyristors

📋 One-Sentence Summary

The exposure system uses a timer to decide the duration and a switching system (mechanical, electronically controlled mechanical, or electronic) to start and stop the X-ray exposure precisely, with modern systems relying on semiconductors such as diodes, transistors and thyristors.

🧠 Central Idea

Timer decides the duration → Switch starts/stops the circuit → X-rays are produced for the required period → Switch stops the exposure.

1️⃣ Introduction to Exposure Timers and Switching Devices

What is exposure?

In X-ray imaging, exposure refers to the length of time that the image receptor remains within the X-ray beam while X-rays are being produced.

  • Overexposure → the film/image becomes excessively dark or “burnt out”
  • Underexposure → the film/image becomes very light or “whitened”

There is therefore an appropriate exposure duration needed to obtain an optimal image. This exposure time is usually very short, making it impractical for the radiographer to control it manually.

🧠 Analogy

Think of using a flashlight to take a photograph:
Shine it for too long → too much light
Shine it for too briefly → insufficient light
Shine it for exactly the right time → a useful image
The X-ray exposure system performs a similar task, but much more rapidly and precisely.

Three things the exposure system must accomplish

  1. Start the exposure
  2. Monitor/control the duration
  3. End the exposure at the desired time
Component Main function
Timing system Determines/monitors how long exposure lasts
Switching system Starts and stops the exposure

Timer = decides WHEN
Switch = allows or stops the electrical flow

What is a switching system?

A switching system works much like an ordinary wall light switch. It can close a circuit (current flows) or open a circuit (current stops). An X-ray switching system receives a signal from the timing system and either opens or closes the electrical circuit.

Location of the switch

The switching system may be located in either:

  • the low-voltage / primary section, or
  • the high-voltage / secondary section

Therefore the material identifies primary (low-voltage) exposure switches and secondary (high-voltage) exposure switching devices.

Types of switches

  • Mechanical contactors — obsolete
  • Electronically controlled mechanical contactors — obsolete
  • Electronic switches
🧠 Page 1 key idea

Timer decides → Switch starts → Exposure occurs → Timer reaches set value → Switch stops

2️⃣ Mechanical and Electronically Controlled Mechanical Contactors

Mechanical contactors

Found mainly in very old X-ray equipment. They are simple devices containing a spring-loaded mechanical contact that closes before exposure begins and opens when the selected exposure time has elapsed.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine an old-fashioned doorbell switch that physically moves. When you press it the contact closes and the circuit is completed; when you release it the contact opens and the circuit is interrupted. A mechanical contactor works using the same basic idea.

Advantages

  • Cheap
  • Simple construction
  • Uncomplicated operation

Disadvantages

  • Poor response time (physical parts must move)
  • Sparking / electrical arcing at the contacts
  • Wear and tear from repeated physical movement
  • Unsuitable for very short exposure times
🧠 Easy memory aid

Mechanical = Moving parts → wear → slower response → possible sparking

Electronically Controlled Mechanical Contactors

These devices combine mechanical contactors with electronic control (using a thyratron valve). The important improvement is that switching can occur when the AC current is at zero value.

What is phased switching?

The AC waveform repeatedly rises and falls. At certain points its value reaches zero. The electronically controlled mechanical contactor is designed to perform switching at this zero point. Switching when the AC current is zero helps eliminate the incidence of sparking / contact welding. This technique is called phased switching.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine trying to stop a moving bicycle. Stopping it when it is already stationary is easy. Trying to suddenly stop it while moving rapidly creates greater stress. Similarly, switching at the zero point of the AC waveform reduces the electrical stress associated with switching.

3️⃣ How the Electronically Controlled Mechanical Contactor Works

The system contains thyratron valves, timer and control circuit, contactor coils, make contactor and break contactor.

Simplified sequence: Timer/control → Thyratron → Contactor coil → Mechanical contact → X-ray circuit

Before exposure

  • Make contactor is open
  • Break contactor is closed
  • Timer/control circuit provides the appropriate voltage bias to the thyratron

When the radiographer selects exposure time and initiates exposure

  1. Timer/control circuit changes the bias supplied to the thyratron associated with the make contactor
  2. Contactor coil becomes energized and produces a magnetic field
  3. Magnetic field pulls the make contactor closed
  4. Make contactor closes → circuit completed → X-ray exposure begins

When the exposure time expires

  1. Timer/control circuit changes the bias of the second thyratron
  2. Second contactor coil becomes energized
  3. Magnetic field causes the break contactor to move away
  4. Circuit is interrupted → X-ray exposure stops

The system is designed so that this switching occurs at the appropriate zero point of the AC voltage.

Why the thyratron is important

The thyratron is an electronic device that controls the operation of the mechanical contactor. It acts as an intermediary: Timer → Thyratron → Contactor → X-ray circuit.

Electronic Switches

Electronic switches do not require moving components. They use the electrical properties of semiconductors. Originally thyratron valves were used; later these were replaced by solid-state semiconductor devices.

Mechanical switch: Physical movement controls current
Electronic switch: Semiconductor properties control current

4️⃣ Thyratron Operation, Primary Electronic Switching and Semiconductors

How a thyratron works

A thyratron has three important terminals: Cathode, Anode and Grid. Electrons normally move from the cathode toward the anode. The grid controls whether this movement can occur.

  • Positive or neutral bias → electrons can flow
  • Negative bias → electrons are repelled and current flow can be stopped

Changing the grid bias allows the thyratron to act like a switch.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine a doorway with a security guard. Guard allows people through → current flows. Guard blocks people → current stops. The grid is like the guard controlling electron movement.

Primary electronic switching

Primary electronic switching involves controlling low voltage and sometimes very large currents. This is achieved using thyristors, particularly Silicon Controlled Rectifiers (SCRs).

What is a rectifier?

A rectifier is a circuit element that allows current to flow mainly in one direction. With alternating current, one half-cycle can pass while the other half-cycle is blocked.

🧠 Analogy

A rectifier is like a one-way road. Cars can travel in Direction A (allowed) but Direction B is blocked. Similarly, electrical current is allowed in one direction.

Semiconductors

Semiconductors are materials whose electrical properties can vary depending on temperature, electric fields, light and impurities. Examples include silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide and silicon carbide.

Doping

A semiconductor can have its electrical properties deliberately changed by adding impurities. This process is called doping. The purpose is to increase the number of charge carriers.

  • N-type semiconductor – a donor impurity adds additional electrons (example: silicon or germanium + phosphorus)
  • P-type semiconductor – an acceptor impurity creates additional holes (examples: doping with boron or indium)

What is a “hole”?

A hole is essentially an absence of an electron where an electron would normally be. The material treats it as a positive space.

🧠 Easy analogy

Imagine a row of seats: ● ● ● □ ● ●. The empty seat □ represents a hole. When an electron moves into that empty position, another position becomes empty. Therefore the “hole” appears to move in the opposite direction.

5️⃣ The P-N Junction and Diode

P-N diode

A simple p-n diode is made by combining a P-type semiconductor and an N-type semiconductor. This creates a P-N junction. The diode can exist in three important conditions: Zero bias, Forward bias and Reverse bias.

Zero bias

When the diode is not connected to an external circuit, electrons near the junction move from the n-region toward the p-region. This movement eventually produces a space charge zone / depletion layer. This layer establishes an electrical field that opposes further electron diffusion. Eventually equilibrium is reached.

🧠 Analogy

Think of people moving from a crowded room into an emptier room. Initially movement occurs easily. As the conditions around the doorway change, a barrier develops that eventually prevents further movement. That barrier is similar to the depletion layer.

Forward bias

Forward bias occurs when the positive terminal is connected to the P-region and the negative terminal to the N-region. The applied voltage pushes the holes and electrons toward the depletion region. If the applied voltage is sufficient, the depletion layer collapses enough to allow charge carriers to cross. Therefore: Forward bias → diode conducts.

🧠 Easy memory trick

Forward = Flow. When forward biased, current can flow.

6️⃣ Reverse Bias and Transistors

Reverse bias

Reverse bias is produced by reversing the external connection (negative terminal → P-region, positive terminal → N-region). This causes holes to move away from the junction, electrons to move away from the junction, the depletion layer to become wider, resistance to increase and current flow to be blocked.

🧠 Easy memory trick

Reverse = Resist. Reverse bias increases resistance and normally prevents conduction.

Breakdown

If the applied voltage becomes sufficiently high, the depletion layer can break down and the diode can then conduct. The material identifies two mechanisms: Zener process and Avalanche process.

Transistors

A transistor is produced by adding another semiconductor layer to a p-n junction. Two basic types are PNP and NPN. The three regions are Emitter, Base and Collector. A transistor can function as a switch or a signal amplifier.

🧠 Analogy

Think of the transistor as a small control valve controlling a much larger flow. A small electrical signal at the base can control a much larger current through the transistor.

Conventional current versus electron flow

Electron flow and conventional current are in opposite directions. Electrons are negatively charged, so their movement is opposite to the direction conventionally assigned to current.

7️⃣ NPN Transistor

NPN transistor structure

An NPN transistor consists of N — P — N. Its regions are: N = emitter, P = base, N = collector. The emitter is heavily doped compared with the base and collector.

Why is the emitter heavily doped?

The base contains holes that can recombine with electrons. If only a small number of electrons were injected from the emitter, many would recombine in the base and very few would reach the collector, making the transistor inefficient. Therefore the emitter is heavily doped so that it can inject a large number of electrons.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine trying to supply water to a large tank through a pipe. If the source provides only a small amount of water, very little reaches the tank. A heavily doped emitter is like a high-capacity water source.

Forward bias of NPN transistor

In forward bias the base is positive relative to the emitter, the emitter is negative relative to the base, and the collector is also positive relative to the emitter (base-collector junction is reverse biased). When the circuit is closed, electrons move from emitter → base; some recombine with holes in the base; most electrons move toward the collector. The transistor therefore enters a conducting state.

Reverse bias

When the connections are reversed, the collector and emitter effectively exchange roles and current from collector to emitter is weaker than in forward operation.

Amplification

One of the important properties of a transistor is its ability to amplify a small current supplied to the base. Applications include transistor radios, fire alarm systems, amplifiers, temperature sensors and logarithmic converters.

Important exam point: A transistor can function as both Switch + Amplifier.

8️⃣ Thyristors

A thyristor can be considered as a semiconductor device with an additional layer compared with a transistor. Its structure is described as P-N-P-N or N-P-N-P. Thyristors are particularly useful as switches for high voltages because of their resilience. A typical thyristor has three junctions: J1, J2 and J3.

Three major operating states

1. Forward blocking state — OFF

Positive terminal → anode, negative terminal → cathode. J1 and J3 are forward biased, but J2 is reverse biased. Because J2 is reverse biased, current does not flow. If the threshold voltage becomes sufficiently high, avalanche breakdown can occur.

🧠 Easy way to remember

Even though the external polarity is forward, the middle junction blocks the current.

2. Forward conduction state — ON

A short positive pulse is applied to the gate. This causes the space-charge region around J2 to break down and allows conduction. An important feature is that the gate does not need to continue receiving the pulse. Once the thyristor has been triggered, it continues conducting (latching behaviour).

🧠 Analogy – locked door

Imagine a door with a special trigger. Door initially locked → no one passes. Press the trigger → door unlocks. Once unlocked, it stays open even after you stop pressing the button. This is similar to the thyristor’s latching behaviour.

How does a thyristor stop conducting?

  • Current falls below the holding current
  • Current direction is reversed (as in AC)
  • Anode or cathode current is momentarily shut off

3. Reverse blocking state — OFF

When the thyristor is connected in reverse bias, current flow is blocked. Therefore it can also function as a rectifier when supplied with alternating current.

9️⃣ Thyristors in the X-ray Circuit and Advantages of Electronic Timers

Thyristors in the mains X-ray circuit

Thyristors are applied to the mains X-ray circuit. Because a thyristor can block current during one polarity of AC, two thyristors are connected in parallel to ensure continuous electrical flow.

Timing control

The timing device supplies current to the gate for switching. When the selected exposure time has elapsed, the timing device stops supplying the appropriate gate current. This controls the switching process and therefore controls the exposure duration.

Phased switching with a pulsing/peaking transformer

The system can also achieve phased switching by feeding current into the gate using a special transformer called a pulsing or peaking transformer. This allows switching to be synchronized appropriately with the AC waveform.

Advantages of Electronic Timers / Switching

  1. Accurate timing – Electronic systems can control the switching time very precisely
  2. No moving components – No mechanical wear and tear
  3. Easy phased switching – Can easily perform switching at the desired point in the AC cycle
  4. Primary and secondary applications – Can be used for both primary/low-voltage and secondary/high-voltage switching
  5. Small control current can control large current – A relatively small gate current can control a much larger anode current

🔑 Very Important Comparison

Feature Mechanical Contactor Electronic Mechanical Electronic Switch
Moving parts Yes Yes No
Speed Relatively slow Better Very fast
Wear Yes Yes Minimal / none
Sparking Possible Reduced by phased switching Avoided / reduced
Status Obsolete Obsolete Modern approach

⭐ Exam-Focused Points

  • Three functions required: Start exposure, Monitor/control duration, End exposure
  • Two major components: Timer and Switching system
  • Three types of exposure switches: Mechanical contactor, Electronically controlled mechanical contactor, Electronic switch
  • Major disadvantages of mechanical contactors: Moving parts cause wear, poor response time, sparking, unsuitable for very short exposures
  • Purpose of phased switching: Switching at the zero point of the AC waveform reduces sparking/contact welding
  • Three terminals of a thyratron: Cathode, Anode, Grid
  • Two semiconductor types: N-type (electrons), P-type (holes)
  • Three diode conditions: Zero bias, Forward bias, Reverse bias
  • Three transistor regions: Emitter, Base, Collector
  • Two transistor types: NPN and PNP
  • Three thyristor operating states: Forward blocking, Forward conduction, Reverse blocking
  • Why two thyristors are used in the mains X-ray circuit: To ensure continuous flow of electricity with AC
  • Five advantages of electronic timers listed above

🧩 One Analogy to Remember the Entire Topic

Automatic water tap

Timer = the person/program deciding how long water should flow
Switch = the valve that opens and closes the pipe
Mechanical switch = a person physically opening the valve
Electronic switch = an electronic sensor controlling the valve without physical movement
Diode = a one-way valve
Transistor = a small control mechanism that can control a much larger flow
Thyristor = a special electronically triggered valve that can remain ON after being triggered until the appropriate condition turns it OFF

So the X-ray exposure system is essentially: Timer decides the duration → electronic/mechanical switch controls the circuit → X-rays are produced for the required period → switch stops the exposure.
