RAD 312

⚡ High-Tension Transformers

Step-up Voltage • Cores • Losses • Regulation • Rating • Efficiency

📋 One-Sentence Summary

The high-tension transformer is the step-up section of the X-ray generator. It uses electromagnetic induction and a large difference in the number of turns between primary and secondary windings to raise the relatively low mains voltage (240 V or 415 V) to the very high voltages (tens to over 100 kV) required by the X-ray tube.

🧠 Overall Flow

Mains (240/415 V) → High-tension generator → High-tension transformer (step-up) → Very high voltage → X-ray tube

1️⃣ Why Does an X-ray Machine Need Such High Voltage?

The ordinary mains supply is far lower than the voltage required by the X-ray tube.

  • Low-power equipment may take 240 V from a wall socket
  • Medium- to high-powered equipment may use 415 V
  • The X-ray tube itself may need 20 kV, 40 kV, or even 140 kV

Therefore the machine needs a reliable method of converting the relatively low voltage into the very high voltage required. This is the job of the high-tension generator, which consists of:

  • High-tension transformer
  • Rectifiers
  • Voltage-stabilizing components
🧠 Analogy

Think of the mains supply as standing at the bottom of a staircase. The X-ray tube needs to be much higher up. The high-tension generator provides the means of climbing from the low voltage to the very high voltage required.

2️⃣ What is a Transformer?

A transformer is a device that can either step voltage up or step voltage down. It contains:

  • An iron core
  • A primary winding
  • A secondary winding

When current flows through the primary winding it produces a magnetic field. This magnetic field passes through the core and induces an electrical current in the secondary winding.

Important principle – Turns Ratio

The voltage depends on the number of turns of wire in the windings:

More secondary turns → higher secondary voltage
Fewer secondary turns → lower secondary voltage

Types

  • Step-up transformer – secondary has more turns than primary
  • Step-down transformer – primary has more turns than secondary

For X-ray high-tension applications we are primarily interested in the step-up transformer.

3️⃣ Transformer Ratio Example (400 V → 120 kV)

Suppose we want to transform 400 V into 120 kV.

Voltage ratio = 120 000 V ÷ 400 V = 300

Therefore the secondary needs 300 times as many turns as the primary.

1 turn on the primary corresponds to 300 turns on the secondary.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine a bicycle with gears. A small gear driving a much larger gear changes the mechanical relationship between input and output. Similarly, changing the number of turns between primary and secondary changes the voltage relationship.

4️⃣ kVp and RMS Voltage

In X-ray equipment, voltage selection is normally expressed as kVp (kilovoltage peak).

Why “peak”? Because the incoming AC voltage does not remain constant. It rises from zero to a maximum value and then falls back toward zero in a sinusoidal pattern.

If an X-ray machine is set to 100 kVp, the 100 kV represents the peak voltage.

RMS Voltage

The root-mean-square (RMS) value is the effective or average AC voltage. For a sinusoidal AC voltage:

RMS ≈ Peak × 0.707

So if the peak is 100 kV, the RMS value is approximately 70.7 kV.

Term Meaning
kV General voltage value
kVp Peak kilovoltage
RMS Effective / average AC voltage

5️⃣ Types of Transformer Cores

1. Air-core type

No magnetic material between primary and secondary windings.

  • Advantage: Can be very small; suitable for circuit-board applications
  • Disadvantage: Not all magnetic flux from the primary reaches the secondary → increased transformer losses
🧠 Analogy

Imagine trying to send water from one pipe to another. If the connection is poorly directed, some water is lost. With an air-core transformer some of the magnetic flux does not effectively reach the secondary winding.

2. Open-core transformer

Uses a rectangular bar core. The secondary winding is wound over the primary winding with insulating material between them. Has lower losses than the pure air-core type.

3. Closed-core transformer

Has a rectangular-shaped core with the windings positioned on opposite ends. The magnetic flux has to travel around the core from the primary side to the secondary side.

4. Rectangular-core variation

Both primary and secondary windings are associated with both sides of the core. The secondary winding is positioned above the primary winding.

5. Shell-type transformer

Identified as the most efficient of the designs discussed. The secondary winding is wound over the primary winding. This arrangement improves magnetic coupling between the windings and reduces energy wastage.

🧠 Easy way to remember

The goal is to get as much of the magnetic field from the primary to the secondary as possible. Better magnetic coupling = less energy wasted.

6️⃣ Magnetic Core, Eddy Currents and Cooling

Magnetic core materials

The magnetic core is made from iron alloys such as:

  • Silicon-iron
  • Nickel-iron

These materials help reduce eddy currents generated by the repeated magnetization of the core.

What are eddy currents?

Eddy currents are unwanted electrical currents generated within the magnetic core as the magnetic field changes. They result in:

  • Heat production
  • Energy wastage
  • Possible damage to components
🧠 Analogy

Imagine stirring water in a bucket. Instead of all the water moving in one useful direction, little circular currents form throughout the water. Eddy currents are similar – unwanted circulating currents develop within the transformer core.

Laminating the core

One method used to reduce eddy currents is to construct the iron core from thin sheets of metal placed together. These thin sheets restrict the paths available for eddy currents and therefore reduce energy loss.

Why does the transformer hum?

The laminated metal sheets are repeatedly magnetized and affected by the AC cycle. The sheets can repel one another during part of the AC cycle and come together again as the cycle changes. This produces the familiar humming sound associated with transformers.

Transformer Oil

Even after measures are taken to reduce losses, the windings and magnetic core still generate a considerable amount of heat. The transformer assembly is therefore immersed in oil.

The oil serves two major purposes:

  • Cooling
  • Electrical insulation

In an X-ray room the high-tension generator tank is an airtight metal container filled with oil, with the high-tension transformer immersed inside it.

🧠 Easy memory trick

Transformer oil = C + I
C = Cooling
I = Insulation

7️⃣ Transformer Losses

A transformer is not ideal. The voltage/power obtained from the secondary is not exactly what would be expected from a perfectly lossless transformer.

There are two major circuits:

  • Electrical circuit – mainly the windings
  • Magnetic circuit – mainly the magnetic core

Therefore losses are divided into electrical losses and magnetic losses.

Electrical (winding) losses

The wire in the windings has resistance. When current flows through this resistance, electrical energy is converted into heat.

Power loss (P) = I² × R
where I = current and R = resistance

How can resistance losses be reduced?

  • Smaller-diameter wires (where appropriate)
  • Shorter wire length
  • Materials with lower resistance such as copper

Magnetic losses

1. Hysteresis loss
Hysteresis produces heat in the magnetic core because the direction of magnetic flux is constantly changing. It can be reduced by using special alloys for the core.

2. Eddy-current loss
Changing magnetic flux produces currents within the core. These currents are dissipated as heat. Eddy-current losses are reduced through:

  • Special alloys
  • Lamination of the core
Loss Main cause Main effect Reduction
Electrical Resistance in windings Heat Suitable wire / material
Hysteresis Repeated magnetic reversal Heat Special alloys
Eddy current Induced currents in core Heat Laminated core + alloys

8️⃣ Transformer Regulation and Efficiency

Transformer regulation

When a transformer has no load connected to the secondary, its output voltage is relatively close to the expected value (little current flows in the primary and essentially no current flows in the secondary).

When a load is connected, current increases and the voltage available at the secondary falls.

X-ray example

As the selected mA increases, the actual potential difference between the cathode and anode can decrease. Therefore a voltage compensator is required to help maintain the selected kV despite changes in mA.

🧠 Analogy

Imagine a water tank supplying several taps. If only one tap is open the pressure is relatively high. If many taps are opened simultaneously the pressure may drop. Increasing the electrical load can cause the transformer’s output voltage to fall in the same way.

Manufacturers describe the voltage drop between no-load and full-load conditions using percentage regulation.

Transformer regulation for radiographic exposures is higher than for fluoroscopy because:

  • Radiography involves an instantaneous load
  • Fluoroscopy operates with a continuous load

Transformer efficiency

Because some energy is lost, the output power is lower than the input power.

Efficiency = (Output power ÷ Input power) × 100 %

Normal transformers have about 90 % efficiency. Larger, higher-rated transformers generally have greater efficiency than smaller, lower-rated transformers.

Example: If input power is 100 units and output power is 90 units, efficiency is 90 %. The remaining 10 % represents energy lost mainly as heat and other transformer losses.

9️⃣ Transformer Rating

Transformer rating refers to the amount of load a transformer can carry for a specified period of time. If operated beyond its capability it can overheat and damage or destroy its insulation.

Why duration matters

  • Fluoroscopy – lower current, sustained/continuous operation
  • Conventional radiography – higher current, short/instantaneous operation

A transformer can tolerate a large amount of current for a very short period more easily than the same current continuously.

Three important rating specifications (expressed in kVA)

  1. Peak kilovoltage – the maximum kV obtainable when the transformer is not on load
  2. Maximum continuous current – the highest current the transformer can provide during continuous operation
  3. Maximum short-duration current – the maximum current the transformer can provide for a period of not less than 1 second

Why can instantaneous current be higher?

During a short exposure the transformer generates heat but has less time for the heat to accumulate to a dangerous level. During continuous operation heat continues to build up.

Therefore radiographic transformers have higher ratings than fluoroscopy transformers because of the different current and operating-duration requirements of the two procedures.

🧠 One-minute revision

The high-tension transformer is the step-up section of the X-ray generator. It uses electromagnetic induction and different numbers of turns to increase voltage. Losses occur due to winding resistance, hysteresis and eddy currents. Laminated cores and transformer oil help control these problems. When a load is applied, output voltage can fall (regulation). Efficiency describes how much input power becomes useful output power, while rating describes how much load the transformer can safely handle for a specified time.
