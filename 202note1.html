SERIES 5: Radiation Interactions with Matter

Why this topic is important: It is the foundation of diagnostic imaging, radiotherapy, radiation protection, detection, and radiobiology.

Two Categories of Radiation

  • Electromagnetic – X-rays and Gamma rays
  • Particulate – Alpha (α), Beta (β⁺, β⁻), Protons, Neutrons

Photon Interactions

  • Photoelectric Effect
  • Compton (Incoherent) Scattering
  • Coherent (Classical / Rayleigh) Scattering
  • Pair Production
  • Photodisintegration

1. Photoelectric Effect

  • Photon energy ≥ Binding Energy of the electron (usually K-shell)
  • Photon is completely absorbed
  • Electron is ejected (photoelectron)
  • Outer electron fills the vacancy → Characteristic X-ray is emitted
  • Probability ∝ Z³ / E³
  • Dominates at low energy and high Z
This is the main reason for contrast in diagnostic X-ray images (bone vs soft tissue).

2. Compton Scattering

  • Photon energy ≫ Binding Energy
  • Photon collides with a “free” electron
  • Scattered photon + recoil electron are produced
  • Wavelength change: Δλ = 0.024 (1 – cosθ) Å
  • Probability depends on electron density (almost independent of Z)
  • Dominates in the diagnostic and therapy energy range (0.1 – 10 MeV)
Compton scatter reduces image contrast and is the main reason we use grids.

3. Pair Production

  • Threshold energy = 1.02 MeV
  • Photon disappears near the nucleus and creates an electron + positron
  • Positron later annihilates → two 0.511 MeV photons

4. Coherent Scattering

  • No energy loss, only slight change in direction
  • No ionization – only excitation
  • Not very important in radiology

5. Photodisintegration

  • Threshold ≈ 8–15 MeV
  • Photon is absorbed by the nucleus → nuclear fragment is emitted
  • Rare in diagnostic and ordinary therapy energies

Attenuation

  • N = N₀ e–μx
  • HVL = 0.693 / μ
  • Linear attenuation coefficient (μ) and Mass attenuation coefficient (μ/ρ)
Q1. Which interaction is completely absorptive and which mainly scatters?
Completely absorptive → Photoelectric Effect & Pair Production
Mainly scattering → Compton Scattering & Coherent Scattering
Q2. Why is Lead a better absorber than Aluminium?
Lead has a much higher atomic number (Z = 82) than Aluminium (Z = 13). Photoelectric effect is proportional to Z³, so Lead absorbs far more efficiently.

SERIES 6: Filtration & Shielding

Filtration

  • Selective removal of low-energy (soft) photons from a polyenergetic beam
  • Hardens the beam (increases mean energy)
  • Reduces patient skin dose
  • Improves image quality and beam penetration

Types of Filtration

  • Inherent filtration ≈ 0.5 mm Al (glass envelope, oil, window)
  • Added filtration – thin sheets of Al, Cu, etc. placed outside the tube
  • Total filtration = Inherent + Added

Common Filter Materials & K-edges

  • Aluminium (K-edge 1.6 keV)
  • Copper (9 keV)
  • Tin (29 keV)
  • Lead (88 keV)
  • Molybdenum (20 keV) – used in mammography
Thoreaus filter (Sn + Cu + Al) is a classic compound filter. High-Z material is placed nearest the tube.

Standard Total Filtration

  • 50–70 kVp → 1.5–2.0 mm Al
  • 80 kVp → 2.0–2.5 mm Al
  • CT → ≈ 4 mm Al
  • Mammography → 0.03 mm Mo

Shielding

  • Purpose: Protect staff, public and sensitive organs
  • Materials: Lead, lead glass, concrete, lead rubber, lead aprons
  • Alpha → stopped by paper/skin
  • Beta → stopped by a few mm of aluminium or plastic
  • Gamma / X-rays → need lead or thick concrete
  • Neutrons → hydrogen-rich materials (water, paraffin) + cadmium/boron
Q3. Differentiate between Filtration and Shielding.
Filtration – removes low-energy photons from the useful beam to reduce patient dose and harden the beam.
Shielding – stops or reduces radiation that is not part of the useful beam (scattered or primary radiation outside the field) to protect people and the environment.
Q4. Calculate the concrete thickness needed to reduce dose from 100 Gy to 0.5 Gy if μ = 0.03 mm⁻¹.
I = I₀ e–μx
0.5 = 100 × e–0.03x
0.005 = e–0.03x
ln(0.005) = –0.03x
–5.298 = –0.03x
x = 5.298 / 0.03 ≈ 176.6 mm (≈ 17.7 cm)

SERIES 7: Luminescence

Luminescence is the emission of longer-wavelength light by a material after it has absorbed shorter-wavelength radiation (X-rays or UV).

Main Types

  • Fluorescence – light is emitted almost immediately (stops when excitation stops)
  • Phosphorescence – light continues for some time after excitation stops
  • Thermoluminescence – heat is needed to release the light
  • Photostimulated luminescence – laser light is used to release the stored energy (basis of Computed Radiography)

How Fluorescence Works in Intensifying Screens

  • X-ray photon is absorbed (preferably by photoelectric effect)
  • Electron is raised to a higher energy level (conduction band)
  • Electron returns to the ground state and emits visible light
Important rule: The emitted light always has longer wavelength (lower energy) than the absorbed radiation.

Key Efficiency Concepts

  • Quantum Detection Efficiency (QDE) – how well the phosphor absorbs the X-rays
  • Photon Conversion Efficiency (PCE) – how many light photons are produced per absorbed X-ray
  • K-edge of the phosphor should be close to or slightly above the X-ray energy for maximum absorption
Q5. Why must the K-edge of a phosphor be considered when choosing intensifying screen material?
Maximum photoelectric absorption (and therefore highest light output) occurs when the X-ray energy is just above the K-edge of the phosphor. Choosing a phosphor with a suitable K-edge greatly improves Quantum Detection Efficiency.
